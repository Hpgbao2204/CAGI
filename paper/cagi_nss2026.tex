\begin{abstract}
Stolen decentralized finance (DeFi) funds are laundered through chained bridge hops, decentralized exchange (DEX) swaps, splits, and mixer or exchange deposits, often within hours of an exploit.
Cross-chain tracers reconstruct these flows after the fact; they do not tell an analyst, while the flow is still unfolding, whether it is heading toward an irreversible exit.
We present CAGI-ED (Cross-chain Attack Graph Intelligence for Early Detection), a CPU-only early-detection pipeline that scores every prefix of a cross-chain fund trajectory.
Its key idea is to type each on-chain event by its DeFi semantics (transfer, bridge, swap, lending, exit) and to summarize the prefix with temporal and behavioral motifs such as split, peel-like chains, and bridge-then-swap pivots.
A calibrated tree ensemble turns these features into a laundering probability and an alert, and each alert carries the features and motifs that contributed most.
On 15 publicly documented cross-chain incidents spanning Ethereum, BNB Smart Chain (BSC), and Arbitrum, with 612 activity-matched hard negatives and leave-one-incident-out evaluation, the typed model reaches a pooled precision-recall area under the curve (PR-AUC) of 0.736.
This compares with 0.688 for the same learner on untyped flat-graph features and 0.457 for a rule-based typology score.
Within individual checkpoints, PR-AUC is 0.933 after observing 25\% of a trajectory.
Platt scaling reduces the expected calibration error (ECE) from 0.039 to 0.011, and scoring a trajectory takes 17 to 29\,ms on a CPU.
In a representative incident, the first alert precedes the confirmed bridge exit by 449\,s.
\keywords{Cross-chain laundering \and Early detection \and DeFi security \and Temporal motifs \and Blockchain forensics}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%=============================================================================

Cross-chain bridges move assets and messages between heterogeneous ledgers and have become core DeFi infrastructure~\cite{robinson2021survey,ou2022overview}.
They are also a preferred laundering route.
Bridge exploits alone account for many of the largest thefts in the ecosystem~\cite{lee2023sok}, and Elliptic estimates that more than \$21 billion of criminal and high-risk funds had moved through decentralized exchanges (DEXs), bridges, and swap services by 2025, a threefold rise from its 2023 estimate~\cite{elliptic2025crosschain}.
In MITRE's Adversarial Actions in Digital Asset Payment Technologies (AADAPT) framework for digital-asset threats, these moves appear as cross-chain hopping, layering, peel chains, and anonymizing services~\cite{mitre2025aadapt}.

Research has made steady progress on reconstructing such flows.
CONNECTOR associates deposit and withdrawal transactions across DeFi bridges~\cite{lin2025connector}.
ABCTracer makes that association bidirectional and self-adaptive using event-log mining and named-entity recognition, reporting 91.75\% traceability over 12 bridges~\cite{lin2025abctracer}.
BridgeGuard detects attack transactions on bridges~\cite{wu2025safeguarding}, and XChainWatcher monitors bridges against cross-chain rules~\cite{augusto2025xchainwatcher}.
Most recently, AMLGuard combines rule-based semantic analysis with retrieval-augmented large language model (LLM) reasoning to trace laundering flows across DeFi and bridges, evaluated on 82 real cases~\cite{wu2026amlguard}.
These systems answer a forensic question: \emph{where did the money go?}
They operate on complete or near-complete histories.

A compliance analyst or bridge operator faces a different question while an incident is unfolding: \emph{given only what has happened so far, is this flow on a laundering path, and how long before it reaches an exit that cannot be undone?}
Answering it requires a model that scores \emph{prefixes} of a trajectory, controls false alerts on the many benign power users whose activity looks similar, and explains each alert well enough for a human to act on it.
Early classification of sequences is well studied in general~\cite{xing2012early}, but, to our knowledge, it has not been evaluated for cross-chain laundering under an incident-disjoint protocol.

Our insight is that the \emph{semantic type} of each step carries most of the early signal.
A bare value transfer and a bridge deposit look alike in a flat transaction graph.
Yet a bridge deposit followed within minutes by a swap into a stablecoin and a fan-out split is a recognizable layering pattern long before funds reach a mixer.
CAGI-ED (Cross-chain Attack Graph Intelligence for Early Detection) therefore types every decoded event and summarizes each prefix with temporal and motif features, and it scores the prefix with a calibrated, CPU-friendly tree ensemble.
It makes the following contributions.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Problem and protocol.} We formalize prefix-based early detection of cross-chain laundering, with lead time measured to a ground-truth endpoint and late detections reported separately (Sect.~\ref{sec:problem}). We evaluate it with a leakage-controlled, leave-one-incident-out protocol and incident-level bootstrap intervals.
\item \textbf{Typed temporal-motif representation.} We define five prefix-safe feature groups: temporal, structural, economic, semantic-action, and motif. The motifs map to MITRE AADAPT techniques, so every alert can be explained in a shared threat vocabulary (Sect.~\ref{sec:method}).
\item \textbf{Evidence on 15 real incidents.} Across three EVM chains and eight bridge families, with 612 activity-matched hard negatives, the typed model attains the highest PR-AUC among rule-based, bag-of-actions, and flat-graph baselines. It is well calibrated after Platt scaling and runs in tens of milliseconds per trajectory on a CPU (Sect.~\ref{sec:results}).
\end{itemize}
The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Sect.~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work, Sect.~\ref{sec:problem} defines the problem, and Sect.~\ref{sec:method} presents the pipeline. Sect.~\ref{sec:setup} describes the data and protocol, Sect.~\ref{sec:results} reports the results, Sect.~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses scope and ethics, and Sect.~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
%=============================================================================

\paragraph{Cross-chain tracing and bridge security.}
Surveys describe the architectural diversity of cross-chain protocols~\cite{robinson2021survey,ou2022overview}, and Lee et al.\ systematize bridge hacks and their losses~\cite{lee2023sok}.
On the security side, Xscope detects bridge attacks with cross-chain invariants~\cite{zhang2022xscope}, SmartAxe finds cross-chain vulnerabilities in bridge contracts by static analysis~\cite{liao2024smartaxe}, and XChainWatcher monitors bridges with Datalog rules~\cite{augusto2025xchainwatcher}.
BridgeGuard characterizes and detects attack transactions~\cite{wu2025safeguarding}.
For tracing, Yan et al.\ match Ethereum--Polygon bridge transfers~\cite{yan2025tracing}, CONNECTOR automates deposit--withdrawal association~\cite{lin2025connector}, and ABCTracer generalizes it to bidirectional tracing across 12 bridges~\cite{lin2025abctracer}.
CAGI-ED builds on this line: it consumes bridge links, each with a confidence, as one event type among several and asks a downstream question those systems do not target.

\paragraph{Laundering detection on account-based chains.}
DenseFlow spots laundering accounts in Ethereum by finding dense value flows~\cite{lin2024denseflow}.
AMLGuard is the closest work to ours.
It parses transaction semantics into DeFi semantic units and uses retrieval-augmented language-model reasoning to reconstruct a compact illicit flow topology across chains, with high destination precision and address recall on 82 cases~\cite{wu2026amlguard}.
CAGI-ED differs in three respects.
It scores \emph{partial} trajectories rather than reconstructing complete ones.
It outputs a \emph{calibrated} probability and an alert time rather than a topology.
It runs on a CPU without a language model, using only public ground truth.
The two are complementary: AMLGuard-style reconstruction can supply the trajectories that CAGI-ED monitors.

\paragraph{Graph learning for illicit-activity detection.}
Graph convolutional networks on the Elliptic dataset~\cite{weber2019elliptic}, its multi-graph extension Elliptic++~\cite{elmougy2023ellipticpp}, and the subgraph-level Elliptic2~\cite{bellei2024elliptic2} established graph learning for Bitcoin anti-money laundering (AML).
Multi-relational~\cite{hyun2023aml}, temporal~\cite{alarab2023graphlstm,patel2022evangcn}, and sequence-pretrained~\cite{hu2023bert4eth,li2022ttagn} models extend it to other illicit activity, and temporal graph networks provide a general dynamic-graph backbone~\cite{rossi2020tgn}.
These methods operate within one ledger, typically on complete histories, and require graphics processing unit (GPU) scale training on large labeled graphs.
With 15 incidents, we deliberately use an interpretable tree ensemble and report where its evidence is and is not statistically conclusive.

\section{Problem Definition}
\label{sec:problem}
%=============================================================================

\paragraph{Events and trajectories.}
Let $\mathcal{C}$ be a set of Ethereum Virtual Machine (EVM) chains.
A decoded event is $e=(t, c, y, u, v, \kappa, q)$: timestamp $t$, chain $c\in\mathcal{C}$, action type $y\in\mathcal{Y}$, source $u$, destination $v$, token $\kappa$, and amount $q$.
The type vocabulary $\mathcal{Y}$ contains seven classes: plain transfer, swap, bridge deposit, bridge withdrawal, lending operation, liquidity operation, and a terminal \emph{mixer-or-exit} class.
Starting from a seed address linked to a public incident, a trajectory $\tau=(e_1,\dots,e_n)$ is the time-ordered sequence of events reached by bounded value-flow expansion (Sect.~\ref{sec:traj}).
Its prefix of length $k$ is $\tau_{1:k}$.

\paragraph{Endpoint.}
The endpoint index $e^\star$ is the first event whose high-risk nature is confirmed by ground truth: a mixer deposit, a deposit to a centralized exchange (CEX), or a bridge exit confirmed on the destination chain.
Its time is $t_e=t_{e^\star}$.
Trajectories whose endpoint is not confirmed by a public source are assigned no endpoint and are excluded from lead-time statistics.

\paragraph{Task.}
For each prefix $\tau_{1:k}$, a detector outputs $\hat p_k=\Pr(\text{laundering}\mid\tau_{1:k})$ computed only from $e_1,\dots,e_k$.
Given a threshold $\theta$, the first alert is issued at $k_a=\min\{k:\hat p_k\ge\theta\}$, at time $t_a=t_{k_a}$.
As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:prefix}, we define
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{lead\_time}=t_e-t_a,\qquad \mathrm{lead\_steps}=e^\star-k_a .
\label{eq:lead}
\end{equation}
An alert with $t_a\ge t_e$ is a \emph{late detection}.
Late detections are reported separately and never averaged into positive lead time.
Because a single lead-time value can be cherry-picked by threshold choice, we report the median and interquartile range (IQR) of lead time and the fraction of trajectories detected before $t_e$ at each threshold.
An alert on a negative trajectory is a false alert.

\paragraph{Threat model and scope.}
The adversary controls one or more pseudonymous addresses holding stolen or illicit funds and may use any public bridge, DEX, lending market, or mixer on the monitored chains.
The defender knows at least one seed address from a public incident report, observes only public on-chain data, and monitors a fixed set of bridges.
Breaking mixer anonymity, discovering unknown incidents by whole-chain scanning, and attributing real-world identities are out of scope.

\subsection{Collection and Decoding}
The collector queries block-explorer application programming interfaces (APIs), namely Etherscan-family endpoints for Ethereum, BNB Smart Chain (BSC), and Arbitrum, for the transactions, internal transfers, and token-transfer logs of each address.
Every raw response is cached as JSON, keyed by (chain, address, incident), together with a checksum and the request metadata, so that any feature value can be traced to the bytes it was computed from.
The decoder maps logs of known bridge, DEX, and lending contracts to the canonical event of Sect.~\ref{sec:problem}.
It unifies protocol naming (for example, Uniswap-style \texttt{Swap} events from different forks), normalizes token decimals, and deduplicates on (chain, transaction hash, log index).

\subsection{Trajectory Construction}
\label{sec:traj}
From each seed, the builder follows outgoing value in time order.
The expansion is bounded by a hop limit and a minimum value fraction \TBD{hop limit and value threshold}, which keeps trajectories focused on the stolen funds rather than on the whole neighborhood.
When a bridge deposit is observed, the builder searches the destination chain for the matching withdrawal using the bridge's event fields.
It records a link confidence of \emph{verified} when an explicit identifier (such as a deposit nonce or message hash) matches, and a lower confidence when the match relies on amount and timing only.
Bridge links were verified on-chain for eight bridge families: Ronin Bridge, Qubit QBridge, FEG SmartBridge, Multichain (AnySwap), BSC Token Hub, LI.FI Diamond, Stargate (LayerZero), and Across SpokePool.

\subsection{Prefix-Safe Features}
\label{sec:features}
For each prefix $\tau_{1:k}$, the extractor computes $\mathbf{x}(\tau_{1:k})$ from $e_1,\dots,e_k$ only.
No feature may read a later event, the endpoint label, or incident-level metadata.
The features form five groups.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Temporal:} time from the first event to the first bridge event, statistics of inter-action gaps, burstiness of the gap sequence, and active duration.
\item \textbf{Structural:} fan-out and fan-in of the addresses in the prefix, density of the ego network around the current frontier, and path depth from the seed.
\item \textbf{Economic:} log amount, the ratio of outgoing to incoming value, and value retention (the fraction of the seed value still traceable in the frontier).
\item \textbf{Semantic-action:} counts of each action type in $\mathcal{Y}$ and bigram and trigram frequencies over the typed action sequence.
\item \textbf{Motif:} counts of five behavioral motifs within a sliding window $\Delta$. \emph{Split}: one address sends to at least $m$ new counterparties. \emph{Merge}: at least $m$ addresses consolidate into one. \emph{Peel-like chain}: a run of transfers, each forwarding most of its input and peeling off a small part. \emph{Bridge$\to$swap}: a bridge withdrawal followed by a swap of the bridged token. \emph{Rapid token pivot}: at least two token changes, typically through a stablecoin, within $\Delta$. We use $\Delta=$ \TBD{window}, $m=$ \TBD{fan threshold}.
\end{itemize}
The semantic-action and motif groups are what distinguish the typed representation from a flat transaction graph.
The flat baseline B3 (Sect.~\ref{sec:baselines}) receives the same temporal, structural, and economic information without event types or motifs.

\subsection{Scoring, Calibration, and Explanation}
The main model M1 is a tree ensemble trained on prefix rows. Each row is labeled positive if its trajectory belongs to an incident and negative if it belongs to a matched benign trajectory.
We use \TBD{XGBoost~\cite{chen2016xgboost} or random forest~\cite{breiman2001rf}: state the learner used for the reported numbers and its hyperparameters}.
Tree ensembles suit this setting: they handle heterogeneous and sparse count features without scaling, train in seconds on a CPU, and admit exact per-prediction attributions.

Raw ensemble scores are not probabilities, and an analyst needs to know whether $\hat p=0.8$ means an 80\% chance.
We therefore compare Platt (sigmoid) scaling and isotonic regression~\cite{niculescu2005calibration}, fitted on out-of-fold predictions of the training incidents only.
An alert reports the calibrated probability together with the top-contributing features and motifs, computed with \TBD{TreeSHAP~\cite{lundberg2020treeshap}: confirm the attribution method}, and their AADAPT technique IDs.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Leave-one-incident-out scoring and first-alert extraction}
\label{alg:eval}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require incidents $\mathcal{I}$ with trajectories, matched negatives $\mathcal{N}$, checkpoints $K$, threshold $\theta$
\For{each held-out incident $i\in\mathcal{I}$}
  \State $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}\gets$ prefix rows of $\mathcal{I}\setminus\{i\}$ and of their matched negatives
  \State fit M1 on $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$; fit the calibrator on out-of-fold scores within $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$
  \For{each trajectory $\tau$ of $i$ and of its matched negatives}
    \For{$k\in K$ in increasing order}
      \State $\hat p_k\gets$ calibrated M1$(\mathbf{x}(\tau_{1:k}))$ \Comment{uses $e_1..e_k$ only}
      \If{$\hat p_k\ge\theta$ and no alert yet} \State record $t_a\gets t_k$ and the top attributions \EndIf
    \EndFor
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\State \Return out-of-fold $\{\hat p_k\}$, first alerts, and lead times by Eq.~\eqref{eq:lead}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
%=============================================================================

We answer four research questions.
\textbf{RQ1:} Does the typed temporal-motif representation detect laundering better than untyped representations?
\textbf{RQ2:} How early can laundering be detected when only 25\%, 50\%, or 75\% of a trajectory is observed, and with what lead time?
\textbf{RQ3:} Can a simple next-action predictor add a useful signal?
\textbf{RQ4:} Is the pipeline practical on commodity hardware?

\subsection{Data}
\paragraph{Positive incidents.}
We collected 15 cross-chain DeFi incidents documented by at least one public security source (CertiK, SlowMist, Beosin, BlockSec, SharkTeam, PeckShield, Chainalysis, or Elliptic).
The 82-case list of AMLGuard~\cite{wu2026amlguard} was used only to locate candidate incidents.
All labels and ground truth were verified independently at the transaction level: seed address, transaction hash, transferred value, cross-chain action, and endpoint.
Each incident's evidence is recorded in an incident registry (Appendix~\ref{app:incidents}).

\paragraph{Hard negatives.}
Benign trajectories were not sampled at random.
Each was matched to a positive incident by chain, time period, transaction value, and activity level, so that the classifier cannot succeed merely by separating dormant wallets from active ones.
After filtering, 612 negatives were usable: 500 from the main mining procedure, 107 from a second mining pass that targets high-complexity multi-protocol activity, and 5 original manually selected controls.

In total, the dataset contains 3{,}354 prefix rows over three chains (Ethereum, BSC, Arbitrum) and eight bridge families.
Prefix rows are extracted at the absolute checkpoints $k\in\{2,3,5,7\}$ and the relative checkpoints $\{25,50,75,100\}\%$ of each trajectory.
The positive rate among prefix rows is 5.0\%, which is also the PR-AUC of the majority baseline B0.



\subsection{Baselines}
\label{sec:baselines}
We compare M1 with four baselines under the identical protocol.
\textbf{B0}, majority/prevalence, predicts the training positive rate and fixes the floor for PR-AUC.
\textbf{B1}, a rule-based typology score, sums hand-set weights for red-flag behaviors (bridge hop, mixer contact, fan-out, rapid pivot); it needs no training and is fully interpretable.
\textbf{B2} applies logistic regression to bag-of-actions counts and tests whether action counts without temporal or structural context suffice.
\textbf{B3} is a random forest on flat-graph features: the temporal, structural, and economic features computed on an untyped transfer graph.
\emph{M1 differs from B3 only in the event types and the motifs}, which is the variable RQ1 tests.
We do not re-run CONNECTOR or ABCTracer as detectors: they solve bridge-level transaction association and produce matched pairs, not laundering probabilities.
Their function corresponds to our trajectory builder's bridge-linking step.

\subsection{Protocol and Metrics}
Evaluation is leave-one-incident-out (LOIO).
In each of the 15 folds, every trajectory and prefix of one incident, together with its matched negatives, is held out, and the model is trained on the rest.
This prevents near-identical prefixes of one incident from appearing in both training and test sets.
Thresholds and calibrators are fitted inside the training folds.
The primary metric is the area under the precision-recall curve (PR-AUC), which is appropriate at a 5\% positive rate.
Uncertainty is estimated with an incident-level bootstrap: incidents, not prefix rows, are resampled, because rows within an incident are dependent.
Ablations report the mean paired difference in PR-AUC, its 95\% bootstrap confidence interval (CI), and a two-sided bootstrap $p$-value.
Calibration is measured by the Brier score and a 10-bin expected calibration error (ECE).
Runtime is the median wall-clock scoring time per trajectory and the peak resident-memory increase, measured on \TBD{CPU model, cores, RAM, OS, Python and library versions}.
Randomness is controlled with \TBD{seeds and number of repetitions}.

\subsection{RQ2: How Early?}
Table~\ref{tab:rq1}(b) reports M1's PR-AUC computed within each relative checkpoint.
With only the first quarter of each trajectory observed, PR-AUC is already 0.933, close to the 0.941 obtained on complete trajectories.
The dip at 50\% (0.849) indicates that mid-trajectory prefixes are the hardest.
We attribute it to benign power users, whose activity at that point resembles layering; the Chibi Finance case below shows the same dip.
The per-checkpoint values are computed on rows from one checkpoint at a time, and each checkpoint has its own positive rate (\TBD{positive rate per checkpoint}).

\subsection{RQ3: A Pre-Registered Data Gate for Next-Action Prediction}
Predicting the next action could, in principle, anticipate an exit.
Before training such a model, we fixed three data-sufficiency criteria.
The data must contain at least 1{,}000 transitions, every one of the seven next-action classes must have at least 30 examples, and the majority class must account for less than 60\% of transitions.
The data pass the first criterion with 26{,}723 transitions but fail the other two.
Two of the seven classes have fewer than 30 examples (the smallest has 4), and plain transfers make up 87.33\% of all transitions.
A next-action model trained on these data would largely learn to predict ``transfer''.
We therefore do not deploy it and report RQ3 as a negative result.
The gate protects against a common failure: reporting a high accuracy that is driven by the majority class.

\subsection{RQ4: Cost on Commodity Hardware}
The pipeline runs entirely on a CPU.
Across trajectory lengths spanning a 248-fold range, the median scoring latency per trajectory grows only from 16.6\,ms to 29.0\,ms, and the peak memory increase stays between 0.145 and 0.155\,MB.
Scoring is thus far below the 12\,s Ethereum slot time, and end-to-end cost is dominated by explorer-API collection rather than by the model.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%=============================================================================

\paragraph{What the evidence supports.}
The comparison with B3, which differs from M1 only in event typing and motifs, supports the claim that semantic typing improves early detection.
The ablations show that this improvement is spread across groups rather than carried by one.
The checkpoint analysis shows that a quarter of a trajectory already carries most of the signal.
Because PR-AUC at 25\% is close to that on full trajectories, a deployment can alert early without paying much in precision.
Lead time and false-alert rates at a fixed threshold (Table~\ref{tab:lead}) determine how much of that advantage survives in operation.

\paragraph{Limitations and threats to validity.}
\emph{Sample size.} Fifteen incidents limit statistical power: several ablations are directionally consistent but not significant, and conclusions about individual feature groups should be read accordingly.
\emph{Selection bias.} Incidents come from public reports, which over-represent large, well-investigated thefts. Negatives are matched, but the matching variables cannot rule out every confounder, and forensic labels may miss attacker-controlled addresses.
\emph{Scope.} The study covers three EVM chains and eight bridge families, starts from a known seed address, and does not scan whole chains for unknown incidents. Non-EVM chains and privacy-preserving bridges require new decoders.
\emph{Mixers.} CAGI-ED detects interactions with mixers but cannot link deposits to withdrawals; post-mixer continuation is out of scope.
\emph{Adaptive adversaries.} An attacker aware of the motifs could interleave benign-looking steps. The low latency makes frequent re-scoring cheap, but robustness to such mimicry was not evaluated.
\emph{Offline replay.} Detection was evaluated by replaying historical data in time order, not on a live stream.

\paragraph{Ethics.}
CAGI-ED uses only public on-chain data and labels from public reports.
It uses no real-world identity information or proprietary exchange labels and performs no de-anonymization beyond those public sources.
It is intended to support compliance and incident response under AML obligations for virtual-asset service providers~\cite{fatf2022targeted}.
Because false alerts can affect legitimate users, alerts are designed as calibrated, explained signals for human review, not automatic enforcement.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%=============================================================================

This paper reframed cross-chain laundering analysis from forensic reconstruction to early detection on trajectory prefixes, and introduced CAGI-ED, a CPU-only pipeline that types every decoded event by its DeFi semantics and summarizes each prefix with temporal and behavioral motifs linked to MITRE AADAPT techniques.
On 15 real cross-chain incidents with 612 activity-matched hard negatives and leave-one-incident-out evaluation, the typed representation attained a higher PR-AUC than rule-based, bag-of-actions, and flat-graph baselines.
It reached a PR-AUC of 0.933 after observing a quarter of a trajectory, produced well-calibrated probabilities after Platt scaling, and scored a trajectory in tens of milliseconds.
A pre-registered data gate showed that next-action prediction is not yet supported by available data.
Together, these results indicate that typing on-chain steps by their DeFi semantics is an effective and inexpensive basis for recognizing laundering before it completes.

Future work will enlarge the incident set so that the contribution of individual feature groups can be tested with adequate power, and extend the decoders to non-EVM chains and additional bridge families.
We also plan to run CAGI-ED on a live event stream and to test its robustness against adversaries who deliberately mimic benign DeFi activity.
Once enough labeled exits exist to pass the data gate, sequence models could forecast the next laundering step.
