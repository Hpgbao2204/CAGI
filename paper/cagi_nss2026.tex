\begin{abstract}
After a DeFi exploit, stolen funds are laundered through fresh wallets, DEX swaps, splits and bridge or mixer deposits, often within minutes.
Cross-chain tracers reconstruct such flows after the fact; they do not tell an analyst, while the flow unfolds, whether it is heading for an exit that cannot be undone.
We present CAGI-ED, a CPU-only pipeline that monitors a seed address, decodes every new action into a DeFi semantic type, expands the tainted value flow, and re-scores the growing trajectory prefix with a calibrated gradient-boosted model whose alerts carry TreeSHAP evidence mapped to MITRE AADAPT techniques.
We rebuilt a dataset of 15 publicly documented cross-chain incidents on Ethereum, BNB Smart Chain and Arbitrum with 514 hard negatives mined from the same bridges, DEXs and mixers, and evaluate with leave-one-incident-out and incident-level bootstrap.
Replaying each trajectory action by action, the calibrated detector at $\theta=0.5$ alerts on 9 of 15 incidents while 2.8\% of multi-action hard negatives raise a false alert; at $\theta=0.3$ it alerts on 14 of 15 at 6.6\% and, where the exit is not the very first action, warns before the exit in three of six incidents, 5 to 114 minutes early.
One minute after the first action, typed features double PR-AUC over the same learner on type-agnostic features (0.39 vs.\ 0.17, paired $p=0.03$); over all prefixes the two are statistically indistinguishable (0.47 vs.\ 0.51), and both far exceed a rule-based typology score (0.20).
Platt scaling reduces the expected calibration error from 0.057 to 0.013, and scoring a prefix takes 12 to 30\,ms.
We release the raw evidence cache, the decoded trajectories and one-command scripts for every number.
\keywords{Cross-chain laundering \and Early detection \and DeFi security \and Temporal motifs \and Blockchain forensics}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%=============================================================================

Cross-chain bridges move assets between heterogeneous ledgers and have become core infrastructure of decentralized finance (DeFi).
They are also a preferred laundering route.
Bridge exploits account for many of the largest thefts in the ecosystem~\cite{lee2023sok}, and Elliptic estimates that more than \$21 billion of criminal and high-risk funds had passed through DEXs, bridges and swap services by 2025, a threefold rise over two years~\cite{elliptic2025crosschain}.
After an exploit, the attacker typically moves the proceeds within hours: fresh intermediary wallets, token swaps into liquid assets, fan-out splits, and finally a bridge deposit, a mixer or an exchange, after which the trail is lost.
MITRE's AADAPT framework names these steps cross-chain hopping, layering, peel chains and anonymizing services~\cite{mitre2025aadapt}.

Research has made steady progress on \emph{reconstructing} such flows.
CONNECTOR and ABCTracer associate deposits and withdrawals across bridges~\cite{lin2025connector,lin2025abctracer}, BridgeGuard and XChainWatcher detect attack transactions and rule violations on bridges~\cite{wu2025safeguarding,augusto2025xchainwatcher}, and AMLGuard combines semantic transaction analysis with retrieval-augmented language-model reasoning to trace laundering paths on 82 real cases~\cite{wu2026amlguard}.
These systems answer a forensic question, \emph{where did the money go?}, on complete or nearly complete histories.
A compliance team or a bridge operator facing an incident \emph{while it unfolds} needs a different answer: given only the actions observed so far, is this flow on a laundering path, and how much time is left before it reaches an exit that cannot be undone?

We call this problem \emph{early detection of cross-chain laundering}.
It differs from forensic tracing in three ways.
The detector sees only a prefix of the trajectory and must not use any information that depends on how the trajectory ends; most prefixes of benign activity look like laundering, so it must be evaluated against benign trajectories that pass through the same bridges at the same time; and its score is only useful if it is calibrated and explained, because a human decides whether to freeze or escalate.

We present \textbf{CAGI-ED} (Cross-chain Attack Graph Intelligence for Early Detection), a CPU-only pipeline that monitors a seed address, decodes every new action into a DeFi semantic type, expands the tainted value flow, and re-scores the growing trajectory after each action with \emph{typed temporal motifs}, features that describe what kind of actions happened, in which order and how fast.
In summary, this paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item We formalize prefix-based early detection with an explicit endpoint, lead time and per-trajectory false-alert rate, and separate online horizons, which a live monitor can use, from relative checkpoints, which are a retrospective diagnostic (Sect.~\ref{sec:problem}).
\item We design an end-to-end architecture from multi-chain explorer APIs to explained alerts, with a bounded value-flow trajectory builder, 37 prefix-invariant features and a calibrated gradient-boosted scorer whose TreeSHAP attributions are mapped to MITRE AADAPT (Sect.~\ref{sec:method}).
\item We rebuild a dataset of 15 publicly documented cross-chain incidents (2021--2025) with 514 hard-negative trajectories mined from the same bridges, DEXs and mixers, and release the decoded trajectories, the raw evidence cache and all scripts.
\item We report a controlled study that holds the learner fixed and varies only the representation, together with online lead time, false alerts, calibration, a mimicry stress test and cost on commodity hardware (Sect.~\ref{sec:experiments}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
%=============================================================================

Bridge security work protects the bridge itself: Xscope checks cross-chain invariants to find bridge attacks~\cite{zhang2022xscope}, XChainWatcher monitors bridges with Datalog rules~\cite{augusto2025xchainwatcher}, and BridgeGuard characterizes and detects attack transactions~\cite{wu2025safeguarding}, building on the systematization of bridge hacks by Lee et al.~\cite{lee2023sok}.
Cross-chain tracers start from the other side: CONNECTOR and ABCTracer associate a bridge deposit with its withdrawal on the destination chain~\cite{lin2025connector,lin2025abctracer}, and their matched pairs are an input that CAGI-ED could consume to continue a trajectory.
Such links are not always recoverable, since privacy-preserving swap protocols such as zk-HTLC are designed to defeat linkability attacks between the two legs of a cross-chain exchange~\cite{tran2026zkhtlc}; this is one reason CAGI-ED treats the bridge deposit on the source chain as the endpoint and aims to alert before it.

For laundering detection, DenseFlow finds dense value flows on Ethereum~\cite{lin2024denseflow}, and AMLGuard, the closest system, parses transactions into DeFi semantic units and uses retrieval-augmented reasoning to reconstruct an illicit-flow topology across chains~\cite{wu2026amlguard}.
Graph and sequence models such as GCNs on the Elliptic dataset~\cite{weber2019elliptic} and BERT4ETH~\cite{hu2023bert4eth} need thousands of labeled nodes on one ledger and score complete histories.
CAGI-ED instead adopts the prefix view of early time-series classification~\cite{xing2012early}: it scores \emph{partial} trajectories, outputs a calibrated probability with its evidence, runs on a CPU without a language model, and is evaluated against hard negatives that use the same protocols.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}
%=============================================================================

A decoded action is a tuple $a=(t,c,u,v,y,\kappa,q,\pi)$ with timestamp $t$, chain $c\in\{\text{Ethereum},\text{BSC},\text{Arbitrum}\}$, source and destination addresses $u,v$, token $\kappa$, log-scaled amount $q=\log(1+\text{amount})$, protocol label $\pi$ and semantic type $y\in\mathcal{Y}$, where $\mathcal{Y}$ holds ten types: \textsf{transfer}, \textsf{swap}, \textsf{bridge\_dep}, \textsf{bridge\_wdr}, \textsf{lend\_dep}, \textsf{lend\_wdr}, \textsf{split}, \textsf{merge}, \textsf{mixer/exit} and \textsf{other}.

\begin{definition}[Trajectory and prefix]
A trajectory $\tau=(a_1,\dots,a_n)$ is the time-ordered sequence of actions reached from a seed $s$ by Alg.~\ref{alg:builder}; its prefix $\tau_{1:k}=(a_1,\dots,a_k)$ is observed at time $t$ for $k(t)=|\{i: t_i\le t\}|$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Early warning]
A detector maps each prefix to a score $\hat p_k=g(\tau_{1:k})\approx\Pr(\text{laundering}\mid\tau_{1:k})$ that depends on $a_1,\dots,a_k$ only.
The endpoint of an incident is its first exit action $e^\star=\min\{i: y_i\in\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{exit}}\}$ at time $t_e=t_{e^\star}$, where $\mathcal{Y}_{\mathrm{exit}}$ contains \textsf{bridge\_dep}, \textsf{lend\_dep} and \textsf{mixer/exit}.
For a threshold $\theta$, the first alert is at $k_a=\min\{k\ge2:\hat p_k\ge\theta\}$, and
\begin{equation}
\text{lead time}=t_e-t_{k_a},\qquad \text{lead steps}=e^\star-k_a .
\label{eq:lead}
\end{equation}
The alert is \emph{early} if $k_a<e^\star$.
\end{definition}

With $\mathcal{P}_e$ the incidents whose confirmed endpoint lies inside the trace and $\mathcal{N}$ the negatives with at least two actions, we report the early-detection and per-trajectory false-alert rates
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{EDR}(\theta)=\frac{|\{\tau\in\mathcal{P}_e: k_a<e^\star\}|}{|\mathcal{P}_e|},\qquad
\mathrm{FAR}(\theta)=\frac{|\{\tau\in\mathcal{N}: \max_{k\ge2}\hat p_k\ge\theta\}|}{|\mathcal{N}|}.
\label{eq:rates}
\end{equation}
The adversary may use any public bridge, DEX, lending market or mixer; the defender knows one seed from a public report and sees public chain data only.

\subsection{Collection, Decoding and Trajectory Building}
\label{sec:traj}

For Ethereum and Arbitrum the collector calls the Etherscan V2 account endpoints (normal, internal and ERC-20 transactions); for BSC it calls NodeReal's \texttt{nr\_getAssetTransfers} in both directions.\footnote{\url{https://docs.etherscan.io/etherscan-v2} and \url{https://docs.nodereal.io/reference/nr_getassettransfers}}
Every response is stored verbatim with its request time and SHA-256 checksum under the key \emph{(chain, address, incident)}, so every feature value is traceable to its source bytes.

The decoder relies on a protocol map $\mathcal{P}$ of contracts whose role was verified on-chain (by event signature or function selector) or in explorer labels: 9 bridge endpoints of 8 families, 6 DEX routers and pools, 2 mixers and 1 lending market.
Legs of one transaction are merged into a single action when the structure is unambiguous: tokens moving in and out of the same address form a \textsf{swap}, one source paying two or more destinations forms a \textsf{split}, and two or more sources paying one destination form a \textsf{merge}; a deposit to a verified bridge, lending market or mixer becomes \textsf{bridge\_dep}, \textsf{lend\_dep} or \textsf{mixer/exit}.

The builder follows the tainted value from $s$.
With $\rho(a)=\mathrm{e}^{q_a}-1$ the raw amount and $O(u,\kappa)=\sum_{a\in A:\,u_a=u,\,\kappa_a=\kappa}\rho(a)$ the outflow of source $u$ in token $\kappa$, it keeps a depth map $d(\cdot)$ with $d(s)=0$ and accepts an action $a$ iff
\begin{equation}
d(u_a)<D\ \wedge\ t_a-t_1\le H\ \wedge\ \Bigl(\frac{\rho(a)}{O(u_a,\kappa_a)}\ge\eta\ \vee\ \{u_a,v_a\}\cap\mathcal{P}\neq\emptyset\Bigr),
\label{eq:builder}
\end{equation}
and then sets $d(v_a)\leftarrow\min(d(v_a),d(u_a)+1)$ unless $v_a\in\mathcal{P}$, so the expansion never walks \emph{through} shared infrastructure.
The share is computed per token so that a large stablecoin payment is not diluted by a small ETH fee.
We use $D=6$, $H=72$\,h, $\eta=5\%$ and $R=2$ frontier-expansion rounds, identical for incidents and negatives.
Traces stay on the incident's primary chain, which makes a bridge deposit a natural endpoint.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Bounded value-flow trajectory builder}
\label{alg:builder}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require seed $s$, protocol map $\mathcal{P}$, depth $D$, horizon $H$, share $\eta$, rounds $R$
\State $\mathcal{F}\gets\{s\}$;\ \ $\mathcal{E}\gets\emptyset$ \Comment{frontier, cached evidence}
\For{$r=1,\dots,R$}
  \State $\mathcal{E}\gets\mathcal{E}\cup\textsc{Collect}(\mathcal{F})$ \Comment{explorer API, SHA-256 cache}
  \State $A\gets\textsc{Decode}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{P})$ sorted by time;\ \ $d\gets\{s\mapsto0\}$;\ \ $\tau\gets()$
  \For{$a\in A$ with $u_a\in\operatorname{dom}(d)$}
    \If{$a$ satisfies Eq.~\eqref{eq:builder}}
      \State append $a$ to $\tau$
      \If{$v_a\notin\mathcal{P}$} $d(v_a)\gets\min\bigl(d(v_a),d(u_a)+1\bigr)$ \EndIf
    \EndIf
  \EndFor
  \State $\mathcal{F}\gets\operatorname{dom}(d)\setminus\{\text{addresses already collected}\}$
  \If{$\mathcal{F}=\emptyset$} \textbf{break} \EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return $\tau$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Prefix-Invariant Features}
\label{sec:features}

For each prefix the extractor computes a vector $\mathbf{x}(\tau_{1:k})\in\mathbb{R}^{37}$ that satisfies
\begin{equation}
\tau_{1:k}=\tau'_{1:k}\ \Longrightarrow\ \mathbf{x}(\tau_{1:k})=\mathbf{x}(\tau'_{1:k}),
\label{eq:invariance}
\end{equation}
i.e.\ two trajectories that share their first $k$ actions but differ afterwards, in content or in length, yield identical features; a unit test enforces this property and caught a length-leaking feature $k/n$ in an earlier version of the code.
With gaps $\delta_i=t_i-t_{i-1}$, their mean $\mu_\delta$ and standard deviation $\sigma_\delta$, three representative features of the three groups are
\begin{equation}
B=\frac{\sigma_\delta-\mu_\delta}{\sigma_\delta+\mu_\delta},\qquad
r_y(\tau_{1:k})=\frac1k\sum_{i=1}^{k}\1[y_i=y],\qquad
\pi(\tau_{1:k})=\sum_{i=2}^{k}\1[\kappa_i\neq\kappa_{i-1}].
\label{eq:features}
\end{equation}

The 14 \emph{flat} features cover tempo (burstiness $B$, $\mu_\delta$, $\sigma_\delta$, active duration $t_k-t_1$), structure (fan-out and fan-in of $s$, counterparties, edge density, hop depth, branching addresses) and value (mean $q$, out/in ratio, retention $q_k/q_1$, tokens holding at least 1\% of the value); they are exactly what a type-agnostic transaction graph offers.
The 16 \emph{typed-action} features are the shares $r_y$ for $y\in\mathcal{Y}$, the number of distinct type bigrams $|\{(y_{i-1},y_i)\}_{i\le k}|$ and trigrams, distinct bridge families, bridge$\to$swap transitions, stablecoin legs and the time to the first bridge action; shares replace raw counts because counts sum to $k$.
The 7 \emph{motifs} are counted only when complete within the prefix: split, merge, peel chain (a run of $\ge3$ transfers with $v_{i-1}=u_i$ and $q_i\le q_{i-1}$), bridge$\to$swap, swap$\to$split, nested bridge and the rapid token pivot $\pi$.

\subsection{Calibrated Scoring and Explained Alerts}
\label{sec:scoring}

The scorer is an additive ensemble of $T=200$ regression trees of depth 4 (XGBoost~\cite{chen2016xgboost}, learning rate 0.1), $F(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_t(\mathbf{x})$ with $f(\mathbf{x})=\sigma(F(\mathbf{x}))$, trained on the prefix rows $(\mathbf{x}_i,y_i)$ of the training fold by minimizing
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}=\sum_i w_i\,\ell\bigl(y_i,F(\mathbf{x}_i)\bigr)+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Omega(f_t),\qquad w_i=\begin{cases}n_-/n_+ & y_i=1\\ 1 & y_i=0\end{cases}
\label{eq:xgb}
\end{equation}
where $\ell$ is the logistic loss, $\Omega$ the usual penalty on the number and weights of leaves, and $n_+,n_-$ the positive and negative row counts of the fold.
The hyperparameters were fixed before any experiment, and features present in fewer than two positive incidents of a training fold are dropped so no feature can memorize one incident.

Raw scores are overconfident, so they are mapped to probabilities by Platt scaling~\cite{niculescu2005calibration},
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\hat p_k&=\sigma\bigl(\alpha\,z_k+\beta\bigr),\qquad z_k=\logit f(\mathbf{x}(\tau_{1:k})),\\
(\alpha,\beta)&=\argmin_{\alpha,\beta}\ -\sum_i\Bigl[y_i\log\sigma(\alpha z_i+\beta)+(1-y_i)\log\bigl(1-\sigma(\alpha z_i+\beta)\bigr)\Bigr],
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:platt}
\end{equation}
where the $z_i$ are inner out-of-fold scores of the training incidents only.
When $\hat p_k\ge\theta$ for the first time, the alert carries the $m$ largest exact TreeSHAP contributions~\cite{lundberg2020treeshap} of the decomposition $F(\mathbf{x})=\phi_0+\sum_{j}\phi_j(\mathbf{x})$, together with the AADAPT IDs of their features.
Because of Eq.~\eqref{eq:invariance}, scoring is stateless and can be repeated on every block (Alg.~\ref{alg:monitor}).

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Online early-warning monitor}
\label{alg:monitor}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require seed $s$, model $F$, Platt parameters $(\alpha,\beta)$, threshold $\theta$, explanation size $m$
\State $k_{\mathrm{done}}\gets1$
\For{each new block on the source chain}
  \State $\tau\gets$ \Call{BuildTrajectory}{$s$} \Comment{Alg.~\ref{alg:builder} on the updated cache}
  \For{$k=k_{\mathrm{done}}+1,\dots,|\tau|$}
    \State $\mathbf{x}\gets\mathbf{x}(\tau_{1:k})$;\ \ $\hat p_k\gets\sigma\bigl(\alpha F(\mathbf{x})+\beta\bigr)$ \Comment{Eq.~\eqref{eq:platt}}
    \If{$\hat p_k\ge\theta$}
      \State $J\gets$ indices of the $m$ largest $\phi_j(\mathbf{x})$ \Comment{TreeSHAP}
      \State \Return \textsc{Alert}$\bigl(k,\hat p_k,J,\textsc{AADAPT}(J)\bigr)$ \Comment{notify the analyst}
    \EndIf
  \EndFor
  \State $k_{\mathrm{done}}\gets|\tau|$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{sec:experiments}
%=============================================================================

We answer three research questions.
\textbf{RQ1}: does typing DeFi actions improve laundering detection over a type-agnostic representation of the same flow?
\textbf{RQ2}: how long before the laundering exit can CAGI-ED alert, and at what false-alert rate?
\textbf{RQ3}: are the alerts calibrated, explainable, robust to mimicry and cheap enough to run online?

\subsection{Dataset and Protocol}
\label{sec:setup}

We collected 15 cross-chain DeFi incidents from 2021 to 2025 documented by at least one public security source (CertiK, SlowMist\footnote{SlowMist Hacked database, \url{https://hacked.slowmist.io/}}, Beosin, BlockSec, PeckShield, Halborn, Chainalysis or Elliptic).
The 82-case list of AMLGuard~\cite{wu2026amlguard} was used only to locate candidates; every seed address, start block and exit was verified on-chain against the public report, and the registry with the URL of every source report (\path{metadata/incident_registry.csv}) is released with the artifact.
For 12 incidents a public report confirms the exit, and for 10 of them the exit action lies inside our trace and defines $e^\star$.

Hard negatives come from the \emph{same} protocols and time window as each incident, because an analyst watching a bridge sees mostly legitimate users.
For each incident we collect every counterparty of the verified bridge, DEX and mixer contracts on its trace within $\pm7$ days and keep it if it interacted directly with the contract, moved a positive value, fanned out to at most three addresses and made no mixer deposit after a non-mixer interaction; a second pass adds complex benign activity, and five manual controls complete the set.
Every negative is expanded by Alg.~\ref{alg:builder} with exactly the same parameters as the incidents.

All raw data were collected through the free tiers of Etherscan and NodeReal in two crawls (134{,}270 cached responses, 3.2\,GB), and a trajectory is kept only if every address it reaches has a complete cache.
The NodeReal quota ran out before all BSC negatives were collected, so 95 of 274 BSC candidates (and one candidate each on Ethereum and Arbitrum) are excluded, and one BSC incident (Paraluni) is rebuilt from our earlier decoded trace of the same seed and window.
Table~\ref{tab:data} and Fig.~\ref{fig:data} summarize the data: incident trajectories are longer (median 40 vs.\ 2 actions) and richer in swaps and bridge deposits, but raw counts and prefix length are not model inputs.

We compare seven detectors.
B0 predicts the training prevalence, B1 is a hand-weighted typology score over motif counts, B2 is logistic regression on the type shares $r_y$, B3 is a random forest on the 14 flat features, and S1 is a GRU over the last 64 typed tokens of the prefix.
B3$'$ is \emph{the same XGBoost learner as M1}, with the same hyperparameters, weighting, filter and seed, on the 14 flat features, so that M1 (all 37 features) and B3$'$ differ only in the representation.

All results use leave-one-incident-out (LOIO): with $\mathcal{G}=\{g_1,\dots,g_{15}\}$ the incident groups, each consisting of an incident and the negatives mined for it, fold $g$ fits every model, calibrator and feature filter on $\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{D}_g$ and scores $\mathcal{D}_g$.
The primary metric is pooled out-of-fold average precision,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{AP}=\sum_{j}(R_j-R_{j-1})\,P_j ,
\label{eq:ap}
\end{equation}
where $P_j$ and $R_j$ are precision and recall at the $j$-th score threshold; we also report its mean over incidents.
Because prefix rows of one incident are dependent, uncertainty comes from an incident-level bootstrap: for $b=1,\dots,2000$ we draw $\mathcal{G}^{*}_b$ from $\mathcal{G}$ with replacement and report the 2.5 and 97.5 percentiles of $\mathrm{AP}(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{G}^*_b})$.
For two detectors $A$ and $B$ we compute $\Delta^*_b=\mathrm{AP}_A(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{G}^*_b})-\mathrm{AP}_B(\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{G}^*_b})$ on the same resample and the two-sided $p=2\min\bigl(\Pr[\Delta^*\le0],\Pr[\Delta^*\ge0]\bigr)$.
Everything runs on a 4-core Intel Xeon (2.1\,GHz, 16\,GB RAM) without a GPU, and every number is produced by one script from one commit.

\subsection{RQ1: Does Typing DeFi Actions Improve Laundering Detection over a Type-Agnostic Representation?}
\label{sec:rq1}

Table~\ref{tab:rq1} and Fig.~\ref{fig:rq1} report pooled PR-AUC over all prefixes.
All learned detectors are far above the prevalence floor (0.046).
M1 reaches 0.471 [0.258, 0.757]: it is clearly better than the typology score B1 ($+0.266$, $p=0.017$), bag-of-actions B2 ($+0.394$, $p<0.001$) and the GRU S1 (0.222, $+0.249$, $p=0.04$).

The comparison that isolates the representation is M1 against B3$'$.
Over all prefixes B3$'$ is slightly higher (0.507), but the paired difference, $-0.036$ [$-0.135$, $+0.066]$, $p=0.69$, is small relative to its uncertainty, and adding typed-action or motif groups to the flat set changes pooled PR-AUC by $-0.045$ and $-0.008$ (both $p>0.6$, Fig.~\ref{fig:3b}).

Weighting incidents equally reverses the order: the mean of per-incident PR-AUC is 0.670 for M1 and 0.608 for B3$'$.
M1 is ahead on New Free DAO (1.00 vs.\ 0.74), UtopiaSphere and WOOFi and behind on Ronin, HackerDAO and DeltaPrime, and the per-incident gap does not correlate with trajectory length (Spearman $\rho=-0.28$, $p=0.32$).
Over all prefixes, typing neither helps nor hurts significantly at $N=15$; RQ2 shows where it helps.

\subsection{RQ2: How Long before the Laundering Exit Can CAGI-ED Alert, and at What False-Alert Rate?}
\label{sec:rq2}

Figure~\ref{fig:4a} scores every trajectory at the online prefix $k(t_1+h)$ for horizons $h$ from one minute to one day, which is what a live monitor sees.
One minute after the first action (median $k=2$ for incidents), M1 reaches PR-AUC 0.394 against 0.172 for B3$'$, a paired gain of $+0.222$ [0.032, 0.402], $p=0.025$, and the absolute checkpoint $k=2$ agrees (0.388 vs.\ 0.161, $p=0.020$).
From five minutes on the two representations are statistically indistinguishable: B3$'$ is numerically ahead between 15 and 60 minutes, once amounts, fan-out and hop depth have developed, and the two are level again from three hours on.
The two early results would not survive a Bonferroni correction over eight horizons, but both views point the same way: event types carry the signal when there is no structure yet.

Retrospective checkpoints $k=\lceil rn\rceil$, which need the final length $n$, confirm the trend (M1 0.480 at $r=25\%$ and 0.652 at $r=100\%$; B3$'$ 0.600 and 0.720).
Figure~\ref{fig:4b} shows why early alerts are possible: the median calibrated score of incidents rises within the first 10\% of the trajectory, while the median negative stays near 0.02.

We then replay every trajectory action by action with the calibrated M1 of its held-out fold (Alg.~\ref{alg:monitor}) and evaluate Eq.~\eqref{eq:rates} for several $\theta$ (Table~\ref{tab:lead}, Fig.~\ref{fig:4c}).
At $\theta=0.5$, 9 of 15 incidents raise an alert and $\mathrm{FAR}=8/287=2.8\%$; the other 227 negatives consist of a single action and are never scored.
Four endpoints are the \emph{first} action of the trace ($e^\star=1$), so no prefix-based detector can warn before them; of the six incidents where an early alert is possible, one (Chibi Finance) is alerted before the exit at $\theta=0.5$.
Lowering the threshold to $\theta=0.3$ raises coverage to 14 of 15 incidents at $\mathrm{FAR}=19/287=6.6\%$, and three incidents are then alerted before the exit: DeltaPrime 114 minutes and six steps early, Chibi Finance 7.5 minutes and 17 steps early, and Radiant Capital 5 minutes and five steps early.
At $\theta=0.7$, FAR drops to 1.7\% with one early alert.

In the Chibi Finance rug pull on Arbitrum (Fig.~\ref{fig:4d}), $e^\star=19$ is a Stargate bridge deposit 10 minutes after the first action.
After the second action the calibrated score is 0.65 and the alert fires 449\,s and 17 actions before the deposit, with the mean log amount ($+1.36$), the seed's fan-out ($+0.90$) and a short mean gap ($+0.62$) as top TreeSHAP terms, i.e.\ AADAPT layering (ADT3028.003).

\subsection{RQ3: Are the Alerts Calibrated, Explainable, Robust to Mimicry and Cheap Enough to Run Online?}
\label{sec:rq3}

We measure calibration by the expected calibration error over ten equal-width bins $\mathcal{B}_1,\dots,\mathcal{B}_{10}$ and by the Brier score,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{ECE}=\sum_{b=1}^{10}\frac{|\mathcal{B}_b|}{N}\,\Bigl|\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}_b|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_b}y_i-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}_b|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_b}\hat p_i\Bigr|,\qquad
\mathrm{Brier}=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\hat p_i-y_i)^2 .
\label{eq:ece}
\end{equation}
Raw XGBoost scores are overconfident (ECE 0.057, Brier 0.061, Fig.~\ref{fig:5b}); Platt scaling (Eq.~\eqref{eq:platt}) reduces them to 0.013 and 0.046, and isotonic regression reaches 0.014.

No single group removal changes pooled PR-AUC significantly (Fig.~\ref{fig:5a}): temporal $+0.025$ ($p=0.86$), motifs $-0.009$ ($p=0.75$), typed actions $+0.029$ ($p=0.79$), bridge context $+0.047$ ($p=0.29$).
Choosing the bridge-context features inside each training split (nested LOIO) yields 0.465, within $0.005$ of M1 ($p=0.63$), so M1 uses no post-hoc feature selection.
With one expansion round instead of two, M1 rises to 0.515--0.598 for $\eta\in\{5,10,20\}\%$ and overtakes B3$'$ (0.473--0.543), so the ranking of the two representations depends on how far the trace is followed.

For mimicry, the attacker follows every action $a_i$ with $j$ decoy transfers of $0.01\,\rho(a_i)$ to fresh addresses, one second apart, which dilutes $r_y$ and the motifs without changing the flow.
The fraction of incidents alerted stays at 60--67\% for $j\in\{1,2,4\}$ (60\% at $j=0$), while the mean score at $r=25\%$ falls from 0.36 to 0.25 (Fig.~\ref{fig:5c}): padding lowers the scores but does not reduce the number of incidents alerted.

Averaged over all prefixes (Fig.~\ref{fig:5d}), the largest $|\phi_j|$ belong to the mean log amount, the bridge-deposit share, the mean gap, the seed's fan-out and value retention, and three typed features and the rapid-token-pivot motif are among the top ten: stolen funds are large, move fast and leave the seed in few hops.
Extracting and scoring one prefix takes a median of 11.8\,ms at $k=2$ and 29.7\,ms at $k=1{,}000$ with less than 0.2\,MB of extra memory, far below the 12\,s Ethereum slot time.
On our synthetic CAGI-Synth benchmark M1 and B3$'$ exceed 0.96 and the random forest 0.93 at every difficulty, so typology-based generators are easier than real incidents and are not used as evidence.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%=============================================================================

Typing pays off in the first minute of a flow and in the explanation, while amounts and tempo dominate later.
The main threats to validity are the small sample (15 incidents, wide intervals), incomplete BSC negatives caused by the free API quota, a bias towards large and well-investigated thefts, traces that stop at the bridge deposit, and an offline replay instead of a live stream with adaptive adversaries.
CAGI-ED uses public data only, performs no de-anonymization and produces signals for human review under AML obligations for virtual-asset service providers~\cite{fatf2022targeted}; the artifact is available at \TBD{anonymous artifact URL}.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%=============================================================================

We framed cross-chain laundering analysis as early detection on trajectory prefixes and built CAGI-ED, a CPU-only pipeline that turns explorer data into calibrated, explained alerts through a bounded value-flow builder, 37 prefix-invariant typed features and a Platt-calibrated gradient-boosted scorer.
On 15 real incidents and 514 hard negatives from the same protocols, it alerts on 9 of 15 incidents at a 2.8\% per-trajectory false-alert rate ($\theta=0.5$), and at $\theta=0.3$ on 14 of 15 at 6.6\% while warning before the exit in three of the six incidents where this is possible, 5 to 114 minutes ahead.
Typed features double PR-AUC over the same learner on flat features one minute after the first action (0.39 vs.\ 0.17, $p=0.03$), are on par over all prefixes (0.47 vs.\ 0.51), and all learned detectors far exceed a rule-based typology score (0.20).
Calibration lowers the ECE from 0.057 to 0.013, cheap decoy transfers lower the scores but not the fraction of incidents alerted (60--67\%), and a prefix is scored in 12 to 30\,ms.

Future work will enlarge the incident set with complete negatives, stitch bridge continuations on the destination chain, and evaluate the monitor on a live stream against adaptive adversaries.
